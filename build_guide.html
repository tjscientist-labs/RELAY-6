1. How to use this guide

The build is split into stages. Each stage ends in a gate: a test that has to pass before the next stage starts. The order puts every electrical risk on the bench first, with a current-limited supply and dummy loads, and only then puts the electronics in a case. Live igniters come late, and a motor comes last.

Build the pad box first. It is the part that can fire a rocket and it is the same for every controller. Then build whichever controller you like first; the other two are new panels on the same core and can follow at any time.

Conventions. Dimensions are millimetres unless marked. "CHECK" means measure the part in your hand before cutting its hole: hole sizes are taken from the makers' drawings where they publish one and are marked CHECK where they do not. References like K1, Q3 and E-2 point at the electrical drawings; P-1 to P-9 are the full-size cut sheets.

2. Overview

A wired launch controller is a battery, a key, a button and a long pair of wires. This design moves the battery and the switching out to the pads and replaces the long wires with a radio link. That buys three things: no 30 m lead to unreel, full battery voltage at the igniter instead of what is left after the wire, and a pad box that can measure each igniter and report back.

3. Safety

This is a pyrotechnic firing system. Treat the pad box as live whenever its battery is connected. Build and test every stage with dummy loads (resistors or lamps), not igniters. Fit real igniters only in Stage 6, outdoors, with no motor. Fit a motor only after commissioning (section 7).

Rules the design follows

The NAR Model Rocket Safety Code and the NAR and Tripoli high-power codes require an electrical launch system with a safety interlock in series with the launch switch, and a launch switch that returns to off when released. They also require a countdown, and a wait after a misfire before anyone approaches the pad. Check the current text of the code that applies to your launch: the wording, the misfire wait and the minimum distances are set there, not here.

  • Interlock in series: the pad box key S2 is in series with the K1 relay coil. With the key out, no current can reach the igniter bus. The controller key is a second, firmware interlock.
  • Spring-return launch switch: every FIRE control is momentary. Releasing it stops the count or the fire command.
  • Fail-safe radio: the pad box drops to SAFE when it hears nothing valid for 1 s, in firmware and in hardware (watchdog U2).
  • Shorted when safe: at rest, K1 ties the igniter bus to ground, so stray voltage cannot appear across an igniter.

Batteries

  • A lead-acid battery can deliver hundreds of amps into a short. Fit F0 (40 A) within 100 mm of the battery terminal and use 12 AWG or heavier from the battery to K1.
  • Charge the pad box with a 12 V lead-acid smart charger, with S1 off. Why not LiFePO₄: small 12 V LiFePO₄ packs carry a protection board that limits current (the 10 Ah ExpertPower: 10 A continuous, 15 A for 10 s). A six-pad salvo or an HPR cluster can trip it and switch the whole pad box off. Use LiFePO₄ only if its board is rated for 40 A pulses or more, with a LiFePO₄ charger (14.6 V).
  • The controller's 18650 cells: buy from a reputable cell seller, use a protected holder or a BMS, and never leave them charging unattended.

Radio

The LoRa modules run in the 902–928 MHz band under FCC Part 15. Use the antenna type the module was sold with and do not add an amplifier. Builders outside the US must buy the 868 MHz (or local) version of the modules and set the region in firmware.

4. Theory of operation

4.1 What a launch system has to do

An igniter is a small resistive bridge, typically around an ohm, that heats a pyrogen until it lights the motor. Model-rocket igniters need a few amps for a fraction of a second; electric matches need less but must have it reliably. A launch system therefore has three jobs: deliver a short pulse of high current to the chosen igniters and nowhere else; make certain that it cannot do so by accident; and tell the operator, before the button is pressed, whether each igniter is connected.

4.2 The pad box: power and arming

The battery feeds two paths through the 40 A main fuse F0. The logic path goes through the main switch S1 to a 5 V buck converter for the ESP32 board, the beacon and the sensors. The pyro path goes straight to the normally-open contact (87) of K1, a 40 A automotive changeover relay.

K1's common terminal (30) is the igniter bus, PAD+. At rest K1 connects PAD+ to its normally-closed contact (87a), which is wired to ground. So in SAFE the bus is not merely switched off but tied to 0 V. When K1 pulls in, PAD+ moves to the battery. An automotive changeover relay breaks the first contact before it makes the second, so the battery is never shorted during the swap.

K1's coil current flows through the arm key S2 and the MOSFET Q7. Q7 is driven by U4 (half a TC4427 gate driver) from the AND gate U3. One input of U3 is the firmware's ARM_EN line. The other is the output of U2, a retriggerable monostable used as a hardware watchdog: the firmware toggles the HEARTBEAT line every time it receives a valid packet, and if the toggles stop for about a second, U2 times out and U3's output falls no matter what the firmware is doing. The pad box can therefore only be armed while all three are true: the key is in ARM, the firmware asks for it, and valid packets are still arriving.

The six gate drivers that fire the pads are powered from PAD+. While K1 is at rest they have no supply and cannot turn a MOSFET on, so a firmware fault in SAFE cannot fire a pad.

4.3 One pad channel and the igniter meter

The igniter connects between PAD+ (red Powerpole) and its own return, PAD−n (black Powerpole). PAD−n runs through a 15 A fuse to the drain of an IRLB3034-type MOSFET (the Infineon original is out of production; the UMW part is a current equivalent) whose source returns to the battery through RS1, the current shunt. To fire, the firmware raises FIRE_n; the gate driver lifts the gate to the 12 V of PAD+ and the MOSFET conducts. A 10 Ω gate resistor and a 100 kΩ pull-down keep the gate quiet; an SMBJ15A transient suppressor clamps spikes picked up by the leads.

Resistance. In SAFE, PAD+ is grounded, so a small current fed into PAD−n flows through the igniter to ground. One Schottky diode from 3.3 V makes a shared TEST rail of about 3.0 V; a 3.30 kΩ, 0.1 % resistor from TEST to each PAD−n sets about 0.9 mA. Two ADS1115 16-bit converters read TEST and each PAD−n. The firmware computes the current from the drop across the 3.30 kΩ resistor and divides it into the PAD−n voltage. At the ±0.256 V range one count is 7.8 µV, which at 0.9 mA is about 0.009 Ω. An open igniter lets PAD−n rise to TEST; a short reads near zero. The reading includes the clip leads, so take it with your leads.

Ignition time. An INA228 reads RS1. When a pad fires, the current steps up; when the bridge burns through, it steps down. The interval is reported as "lit in 184 ms". A pad whose current never drops within the pulse is reported as NO FIRE at once, before anyone walks out.

4.4 The radio link

LoRa radios already add a CRC to every packet and discard any that arrive damaged. A CRC proves only that a packet was not corrupted; anyone can compute one. So every packet also carries an 8-byte AES-128-CMAC tag computed with a key shared only by the paired controller and pad box, and a 32-bit counter that must increase. A packet with a bad tag or an old counter is dropped without a reply.

Arming is a challenge and response: the pad box answers an ARM request with a fresh random nonce, valid for 30 s, and accepts ARM and FIRE only if they carry that nonce under a valid tag. A recording of last week's FIRE packet is therefore useless. Pairing writes a new random key into both units over USB (or by an NFC tap); there is no default key, and an unpaired unit will not arm.

4.5 The controller

The controller is a Heltec WiFi LoRa 32 V3 (ESP32-S3 with an SX1262 radio) plus a 3.12-inch 256 × 64 OLED, a rotary encoder, a key switch, an NFC reader for the range-safety officer's card, an I²S amplifier and speaker for the spoken countdown, a GPS for the log, and one chain of WS2812 LEDs for all the panel lights. It runs from two 18650 cells in parallel through a USB-C charger and a 5 V boost converter. The E-STOP is in series with the battery: pressing it kills the controller, the pad box hears no heartbeat, and it drops to SAFE within a second.

4.6 Firmware

Both units run one firmware tree (PlatformIO, Arduino-ESP32 with RadioLib for the SX1262 and the ESP32-S3's AES hardware for CMAC), built twice with a role flag. The pad box firmware is the authority: it owns the state machine in E-6, and the controller can only ask. Timing constants are collected in section 11. Firmware Rev A (firmware/): the core logic (AES-128-CMAC, packets, both state machines, the Ω meter and ignition timer) is plain C++ and passes 71 host tests, including a recorded-packet replay attack, link loss, a pulled key and a welded K1; all four targets compile for the Heltec V3. It has not yet run on hardware, so stages 1–5 are where it meets real boards. The radio runs on one 915 MHz channel at SF8 and 500 kHz bandwidth: for a single-channel (non-hopping) system in 902–928 MHz, FCC 15.247 requires at least 500 kHz of bandwidth. Check the rules that apply to you.

Tools

6. The build, stage by stage

Thirteen stages. 0–6 are on the bench and prove the electronics; 7–8 put the pad box in its can; 9–11 build the three controllers (any order, any one first); 12 is the first field check. Section 7 (commissioning) follows.

Stage 0 · Order and measure

  1. Order the pad box and core parts from section 5 and one controller's panel parts. Buy two Heltec V3 boards per pair plus one spare.
  2. When the case arrives, measure its inside: length and width at the rim of the base, depth of base and lid, corner radius, and how far the gasket groove stands in. Enter them in src/parts.py and regenerate: every panel outline, hole and DXF is drawn from those numbers.
  3. Measure every CHECK part (key switches, e-stop, arcade buttons, toggles, Powerpole bracket, OLED module) and correct its hole on the cut sheets.
Gate 0. Every CHECK (red) on P-1 to P-9 confirmed or changed; the panel outline fits the case with 1 mm clearance all round.

Stage 1 · Two radios talking

  1. Flash both Heltec boards with a RadioLib "ping-pong" test at 915 MHz (or your region), SF7/125 kHz, CRC on. Antennas fitted before any transmit.
  2. Add the authentication layer: a 16-byte test key, AES-128-CMAC over header and payload, 8-byte tag, 32-bit counter. Log every rejected packet with its reason.
  3. Tamper tests: flip one payload bit (must fail the tag), replay a captured packet (must fail the counter), use a different key (must fail the tag).
Gate 1. 1000 packets each way across the room with none rejected; every tamper test rejected; a power-cycled unit resumes with a higher counter (counter kept in flash).

Stage 2 · Controller core on the bench

  1. On a full-size proto board: Heltec V3 in sockets, 74AHCT125 level shifter, MCP23017 (or the ADS1115 for MONOLITH), headers for the OLED, PN532, MAX98357A and GPS. Wire per E-4.
  2. Check the proposed GPIO map in section 11 against Heltec's V3 pinout before soldering; change the table, not the board, if it disagrees.
  3. Power from the bench supply at 3.7 V, 1 A limit, through the boost converter. Bring up one module at a time: OLED test pattern, encoder counts, PN532 reads a card, a WAV clip plays, GPS gets a fix outdoors, an 8-LED test strip lights.
Gate 2. Every module works together on the one board; current under 400 mA with the LEDs dim; the OLED draws the six screens from the concept sheet with test data.

Stage 3 · Pad box logic and watchdog

  1. Second proto board: Heltec V3, buck converter, U2, U3, U4 and Q7 per E-2. Use a 12 V bench supply at 1 A limit in place of the battery. Fit K1 in its socket but connect nothing to 30, 87 or 87a yet.
  2. Set U2's timing: start with 100 kΩ and 10 µF; toggle HEARTBEAT from a test sketch, then stop. Measure, with a scope or the ESP32's own timer, how long U2's Q stays high after the last edge. Adjust C until it is 0.8–1.2 s and record it in section 11.
  3. Truth table: key OFF / ON × ARM_EN low / high × heartbeat running / stopped. K1 must pull in only for ON + high + running.
  4. Crash test: with K1 pulled in, make the sketch hang in a loop. K1 must drop within the watchdog time.
Gate 3. All eight rows of the truth table correct; K1 drops on a firmware hang; with S1 off K1 cannot pull in.

Stage 4 · Six channels into dummy loads

  1. Build the six channels per E-3 on the power board: the six IRLB3034-type MOSFETs on a common aluminium bar (insulating pads), fuse holders, TVS diodes, gate drivers U5–U7. PAD+ and the return in 12 AWG; each channel in 14 AWG.
  2. Wire K1: 87 to the fused battery feed, 87a to ground, 30 to the PAD+ bus. Check with the meter, power off, that 30 reads 0 Ω to ground.
  3. Six 12 V 21 W lamps on the outputs, bench supply at 12 V, 10 A limit. Arm and fire each pad, then salvo. Each lamp lights for 1.0 s only.
  4. With K1 at rest, command FIRE on every pad from the test sketch: nothing must light (the gate drivers have no supply).
Gate 4. Every pad fires only when armed; no pad lights with K1 at rest whatever the firmware does; MOSFET bar warm at most after a six-lamp salvo.

Stage 5 · Igniter meter calibration

  1. Fit the TEST rail (D0), the six 3.30 kΩ resistors, the input resistors and clamps, and the two ADS1115s.
  2. Put 0.50 Ω, 1.00 Ω and 2.00 Ω (0.1 % or measured on a 4-wire meter) on each pad in turn. Record the readings; store a per-channel offset for lead and contact resistance in flash.
  3. Open circuit reads OPEN; a short reads below 0.2 Ω.
  4. Measure the actual test current with a meter in series: it should be close to 0.9 mA.
Gate 5. All six pads read the reference resistors within ±0.05 Ω; OPEN and SHORT detected.

Stage 6 · Real igniters, no motor

Outdoors, no motor, igniters only. Stand well back as for a launch. Confirm from the igniter's data sheet that the 0.9 mA test current is far below its no-fire current before connecting it. If it is not, reduce it (raise the 3.30 kΩ resistors) and repeat Stage 5.
  1. Battery in place of the bench supply, F0 fitted. Igniters on all six pads.
  2. Fire each mode from the controller. Check the FIRED report gives a time for each pad.
  3. Fire a three-igniter cluster on one pad. Read the current from the INA228 log; it must stay under the 15 A pad fuse.
Gate 6. Every igniter lit; every ignition time reported; no fuse blown; battery voltage recovers after a salvo; the INA228 log shows the salvo peak current, and the pad box stayed powered through it.

Stage 7 · The pad box can

  1. Tape the 1:1 templates P-8 (front) and P-9 (lid) to the can and centre-punch: six Powerpole outputs and the key on the front, the mast socket, antenna bulkhead and anemometer post on the lid, charge port and main switch on the side.
  2. Drill and punch, deburr, then paint the inside of every hole to stop rust. Fit grommets and the PG7 gland.
  3. Fit an inner plate (6 mm plywood or HDPE) carrying the battery strap, the power board and the logic board. The battery sits low and is strapped; nothing rests on it.
Gate 7. The lid closes over the wiring with the gasket seated; every hole is deburred and sealed; the board plate is fixed at four points.

Stage 8 · Pad box assembly

  1. Move the boards into the can. Re-run Gates 3, 4 and 5 in the can.
  2. Fit the beacon mast (two 12 in lengths of ½ in aluminium tube joined by a short internal coupler, plugged into the lid socket) and the anemometer. Check the WIND reading against a hand anemometer or a weather report.
  3. Label every output 1–6, red +, black −, and the key positions SAFE / ARM.
Gate 8. Gates 3–5 pass again with the lid closed; beacon visible from 50 m in daylight.

Stage 9 · HALO

  1. Cut the HALO panel (P-1, 3 mm black acrylic), the lid insert (P-2) and the ring, flip cover and hinge blocks (P-3: 6 mm clear, 3 mm clear, 6 mm black). Sand the ring's bottom face so it diffuses.
  2. Glue the WS2812 strip in a ring under the four arc slots, LEDs facing up; bond the clear ring to the four bridges on top with acrylic cement. Fit the six 30 mm buttons with a NeoPixel mini PCB in each, the 60 mm FIRE button, the cover microswitch, key switch, e-stop, encoder and NFC window.
  3. Panel on four standoffs from the case wall cleats; core board under it. Lid: OLED in its bezel, speaker, antenna bulkhead in the case wall.
Gate 9. Ring lights evenly with no hot spots; FIRE does nothing with the cover closed; full Stage 4 lamp test from the HALO.

Stage 10 · FLIGHTDECK

  1. Order the aluminium panel from P-4 (laser cut, powder coated) or cut 3 mm acrylic for a first version; the lid insert is P-5.
  2. Fit the six covered toggles, the six NeoPixel Stick-8 status bars behind a frosted strip, the ARM and FIRE buttons as far apart as the panel allows (252 mm centres), the master key, e-stop and mode encoder.
  3. ARM and FIRE are wired in series into the one FIRE input: either released, the input opens. Lid: OLED and the checklist card over an edge-lit acrylic sheet.
Gate 10. Neither button alone starts a count; a lifted cover shows its pad in the selection; full lamp test.

Stage 11 · MONOLITH

  1. Cut the panel from P-6 as two layers of 3 mm black acrylic and laminate them (or 6.9 mm acetal from a CNC service); lid insert P-7. Confirm the slide-pot lever width before cutting the slots.
  2. Fit the six slide potentiometers under the slots with machined or printed lever caps; one WS2812 strip under each slot. "Committed" is the top 10 % of travel.
  3. Fire core: the 30 mm illuminated button sits in the 6810 bearing's bore; a printed collar on the bearing carries a cam that closes the COVER microswitch at 90°, and a torsion spring returns it. Twin OLEDs in the lid share SPI with separate chip selects.
Gate 11. Collar returns to SAFE on its own from any angle; levers read cleanly end to end; full lamp test.

Stage 12 · Field check

Run section 7 in full before the first motor.

Gate 12. Every item in section 7 ticked and signed by a second person.

7. Commissioning

Do this at a club launch or a private field you are permitted to fly at, with a range-safety officer, before the first flight.

  • Pair the controller and the pad box with a new key. Confirm an unpaired controller is ignored (OLED shows no link).
  • Range test with the antennas as they will be used: walk the controller out to twice your planned distance. RSSI and SNR on the LINK screen stay above the margin in section 11.
  • Six lamps (12 V, 21 W automotive) on the six outputs. Fire each mode: single, select, salvo, ripple at 0.5 s, drag race. The lamps flash in the expected order and the FIRED report matches.
  • Walk away until the link drops; the beacon turns green and K1 releases within 1 s.
  • Pull the pad key while ARMED: K1 releases at once and the controller shows the key fault.
  • Press the controller E-STOP while ARMED: the pad box returns to SAFE within about 1 s.
  • Fire one real igniter with no motor on each pad, then a cluster of three on one pad. Ignition times are reported.
  • Only then: first motor, single pad, low-power rocket, full countdown.

8. At the field

Setting up

  1. Pad box to the rack, mast up, antenna on, S1 ON. Beacon green.
  2. Clip leads on, one per pad. The controller's PAD screen shows each igniter's resistance; fix anything outside the window now.
  3. Back at the line: controller on, link shown, wind under the limit.

Flying

  1. RSO card on the NFC pad (if used). Key ON. Select pads and mode.
  2. Walk out, turn the pad box key to ARM, walk back. (The pad key is the field's interlock: whoever holds it controls the rack.)
  3. Call the range. Hold FIRE (both hands on FLIGHTDECK). The count runs on the speaker and the OLED; releasing FIRE stops it.
  4. Read the FIRED report. Any NO FIRE: key the pad box to SAFE, wait the code's misfire time, then approach.

Packing up

Pad key out first. S1 off. Leads coiled. The log is on the controller's microSD (or as a QR code on the OLED).

10. Open items

  • Firmware. Rev A written and host-tested; not yet run on hardware. Still to do: voice clips (beeps only now), the GPS log, NFC RSO cards, the microSD log and QR code, and on-air range and timing checks.
  • Heltec V3 pin map. The GPIO assignments in section 11 are proposals; check each against Heltec's pinout (pins used by the radio, the on-board OLED and flash are not free).
  • Igniter test current. 0.9 mA was chosen so the meter can read ohms. Confirm it is far below the no-fire current of the igniters and e-matches you will use (maker's data sheet) before Stage 6.
  • Watchdog timing. Set U2's R and C on the bench for 0.8–1.2 s and record the measured value.
  • Case sizes. Apache 3800 interior 14-7/8 × 10-5/8 × 6-1/8 in (377.8 × 269.9 × 155.6 mm) is Harbor Freight's published figure; it does not give the lid/base split, so 44 / 111 mm is taken from the Pelican 1450 it copies (lid 1.75 in, base 4.37 in). The corner radius (14 mm) and the ammo-can sizes are estimates. Measure the real cases in Stage 0 and edit parts.py.
  • Range. No range figure is claimed. Measure it (section 7) with the antennas you will use.
  • A PCB. Revision A is built on proto boards. A carrier PCB for each unit would make it easier for others to copy; it is a later revision.
